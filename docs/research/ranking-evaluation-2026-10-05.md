# Ranking evaluation for KINA (2026-10-05)

Question: how should KINA rank distributor candidates against a free-text request? This report evaluates the
deterministic ranker and the alternatives that remain relevant to the product on a labelled set of 32 requests and
1259 real candidates. Every number below comes from a script in `scripts/research/` (see its `README.md`); score files
for every method are committed in `scripts/research/out/scores/`, and every table is generated by `report_tables.py`
(from `evaluate.py` and `select_cv.py`) and copied into this file by `fill_report.py`.

## 1. Summary

- **The deterministic ranker is strong and nearly free.** NDCG@10 0.892. It is near perfect on passives (0.988) and
  good on discretes, ICs and connectors (0.902 to 0.930), but weak on vague requests (0.620). It runs in a few
  milliseconds for 40 candidates. Learned weights on its features do not beat the hand-set ones (0.868 / 0.876 under
  leave-one-query-out).
- **Distributor order and BM25 are clearly worse** (0.800 and 0.745).
- **A small cross-encoder adds a real, modest gain as a second signal.** `cross-encoder/ms-marco-MiniLM-L6-v2`
  (22.7 M parameters, Apache-2.0) is 0.874 alone. A 50/50 rank-normalised blend with the deterministic score gives 0.913
  (CI of the difference to the deterministic ranker +0.003 to +0.041). This is what KINA ships. Fine-tuned on
  synthetic rubric labels plus half the real labels, the blend gives 0.917 (CI +0.007 to +0.045), and the gain holds
  after leave-one-query-out selection of the blend (0.917).
- **Bi-encoders and larger cross-encoders do not help more.** Bi-encoders are 0.777 to 0.790 alone; the 12x larger
  `bge-reranker-base` blends to the same 0.913 as MiniLM-L6 at about 7x the latency.
- **The cross-encoder fits the latency budget easily.** ONNX Runtime int8: 85 ms median, 199 ms worst for a query's
  candidates on 8 threads, with the same accuracy as fp32 (0.877 vs 0.874 alone).
- **A decision-model ranking sidecar was also evaluated in the original study.** On its own it scored below the
  deterministic ranker in every configuration tried (zero-shot and fine-tuned); as a blend partner it never matched the
  cross-encoder blend, at seconds of CPU per query and a separate multi-GB service. It was removed from the product,
  and its scorers, score files and sections were removed from this report; that material is in the git history
  (`git log -- docs/research scripts/research`).
- **Recommendation (shipped):** keep the deterministic ranker primary and add the MiniLM cross-encoder as a second
  signal in a 50/50 rank blend, in-process through ONNX Runtime for Java, with the deterministic order as the fallback.
  Section 9 has the details.

## 2. Methodology

### 2.1 Dataset

`docs/research/data/ranking-eval.jsonl`, documented in `docs/research/data/README.md`. The file now holds 41 queries
(1619 candidates): the 32 queries of this study plus 9 USB connector queries (`u01`-`u09`) added later. The committed
score files, and therefore every table here, cover the 32 study queries; `evaluate.py` scores a method on the queries
its score file contains.

- 32 requests: 10 passive, 6 discrete, 7 IC, 4 crystal/connector, 5 vague natural-language.
- 1259 candidates, 20 to 40 per query. Labels: 0 = 166, 1 = 342, 2 = 292, 3 = 459.
- Distributors: LCSC 1004, Mouser 170, TME 85.
- Sources:
  - 413 candidates from the running KINA stack (15 stack queries, 13 of them new).
  - 289 from KINA's own LCSC retrieval for the query text.
  - 557 hard negatives mined with KINA's LCSC retrieval for perturbed queries, from the full 7.1 M-part JLCPCB database.

Relevance scale, applied through a written rubric per query (stored in each record):

| label | meaning |
|---|---|
| 3 | meets every stated requirement (for vague requests: the interpretation written before scoring) |
| 2 | right type and primary value or function; one secondary requirement violated, or requirements not verifiable from the part data |
| 1 | same broad family but wrong primary value, two or more violations, or a near family (tantalum for MLCC, resistor array, other diode type, comparator for op amp) |
| 0 | different component type |

Labels were produced by rubric functions written independently of KINA's Java parsers (`scripts/research/queries.py`).
Every label was then reviewed by hand, and 10 corrections were recorded with reasons. The vague-query rubrics were
written before any method was scored.

### 2.2 Metrics

`scripts/research/evaluate.py` computes each metric per query, then takes the mean over the queries.

| metric | definition |
|---|---|
| NDCG@5, NDCG@10 | gain 2^label - 1 |
| P@3 | share of the top 3 with label 3 |
| MRR | reciprocal rank of the first label-3 candidate |
| Spearman | rank correlation between the method's scores and the labels |

Ties in a method's scores are broken by a fixed hash of the candidate key, the same for every method.

For each method, the difference in NDCG@10 against the deterministic ranker has a paired bootstrap 95% confidence
interval (10 000 query resamples, seed 7).

Many hybrids were tried on the same queries, so the best one is optimistically biased. `select_cv.py` therefore also
reports a leave-one-query-out selection: the configuration is chosen on all other queries and scored on the held-out
one.

### 2.3 Hardware and latency protocol

- **Host:** Intel Core Ultra 9 285K, 8 P-cores (cpus 0-7) and 16 E-cores (8-23), 30 GB RAM.
- **Accuracy runs:** done in parallel with other experiments; sentence-transformers scorers and fine-tunes ran on
  E-cores and are marked `_ecore`. Their latency is not comparable.
- **Latency runs:** latency numbers in the tables come from `latency.py`, re-run on P-cores with nothing else running:
  8 threads on cpus 0-7 and 4 threads on cpus 0-3. A measurement is the scoring of every candidate of a query from
  scratch (query and candidate encoding, no cache).
- **RAM:** the peak RSS of the scoring process (Python, so it includes the interpreter, torch and transformers).

### 2.4 Methods

| group | methods | implementation |
|---|---|---|
| a. baselines | `det` (production `DeterministicRanker`), distributor order, BM25 | `score_baselines.py`; `det` calls the real Java classes through `ResearchRunner` (a JUnit-free runner in package `ro.alacrity.kina.search`). It decomposes each score into a feature vector, and the runner aborts if the parts do not add back up to the production score. |
| b. learned weights | ridge on the label and pairwise logistic over the 10 deterministic features, leave-one-query-out | `score_learned.py` |
| c. bi-encoders | `all-MiniLM-L6-v2`, `bge-small-en-v1.5`: cosine of query and candidate embeddings | `score_neural.py` |
| d. cross-encoders | `ms-marco-MiniLM-L6-v2`, `ms-marco-MiniLM-L12-v2`, `bge-reranker-base` (PyTorch); MiniLM-L6 in ONNX Runtime fp32 and dynamic int8 | `score_neural.py`, `score_onnx.py` |
| e. fine-tuning | MiniLM-L6 cross-encoder on real labels (2-fold), synthetic labels, and synthetic + real labels (2-fold) | `ce_finetune.py`, `synth_data.py` |
| f. hybrids | additive formula `(1-w) det + w ranknorm(X)`; rank blend `(1-w) ranknorm(det) + w ranknorm(X)`, both for w in {0.1, 0.2, 0.3, 0.5}; X only breaks exact ties; X inside deterministic bands of 0.05; X re-orders the deterministic top 10; X on candidates with det >= 0.5 | `score_hybrids.py` |

`ranknorm` is a dense rank scaled to [0, 1] (best 1.0, ties share a value), as in `RankingService`.

**Which deterministic ranker.** `det` is not a re-implementation. It is the production Java code as of commit
`af48f11` (branch base `00d6978`), compiled from `src/main`. While this study ran, `main` gained connector-aware parsing
and ranking (commit `bf1d59b`). That revision is also scored, as `det_main_bf1d59b`, by `score_det_ref.py`: it compiles
`git archive bf1d59b src/main` and runs the same `ResearchRunner`. All other `det` rows use `af48f11`.

The 2-fold split is by query and stratified by category (`common.folds`). Each fold trains on one half and is
evaluated on the other, so every query is scored by a model that never saw it.

The synthetic data comes from `synth_data.py`: 80 random passive requests, 2872 candidates retrieved by KINA's LCSC
search, labelled by the same rubric functions. Value and package combinations used by the evaluation queries are
excluded.

What the categories show:

- **Passives:** the deterministic ranker is essentially solved here (0.988). Zero-shot neural models lose points by
  ranking a part with the right value but the wrong voltage or dielectric above an exact match. A cross-encoder trained
  on synthetic rubric labels plus real labels reaches 0.992 on its own.
- **Discretes, ICs, crystals and connectors:** the zero-shot cross-encoder is better than the deterministic ranker
  (discrete 1.000 vs 0.902, IC 0.957 vs 0.930, crystal/connector 0.967 vs 0.910). These requests depend on words the
  parametric parser does not model: RS-485 vs CAN, `1x4P`, unidirectional, a Zener nominal voltage among ranges. The
  connector-aware deterministic ranker of `bf1d59b` closes part of the connector gap (0.955).
- **Vague requests:** every method is weak, and the deterministic ranker is the least bad (0.620). The cross-encoder
  alone drops to 0.402. The 50/50 rank blend stays close to the deterministic level (0.603; 0.611 with the fine-tuned
  cross-encoder) while gaining everywhere else.

What the table shows:

- **Cross-encoders beat bi-encoders here.** The cross-encoder reads the query and the part together, so it can check
  "16 pin" against "16P" or "RS485" against "CAN"; a bi-encoder only compares two separate embeddings (0.777 and 0.790
  alone, against 0.851 to 0.874 for the cross-encoders).
- **The smallest cross-encoder is as good as the 12x larger `bge-reranker-base`** in the blend (0.913 for both), and
  MiniLM-L12 adds nothing over L6 (0.873 vs 0.874 alone, 0.907 vs 0.913 blended). The extra size buys nothing.
- **ONNX Runtime reproduces PyTorch.** The fp32 export ranks every candidate exactly as PyTorch does (Spearman 1.0000
  over all candidates, identical metrics); dynamic int8 quantisation agrees at Spearman 0.9981 and scores 0.877 alone
  (section 8 for latency).

## 6. Fine-tuning the cross-encoder

Recipe (`ce_finetune.py`): pairs `(query, candidate_text)` with soft target `label / 3`, BCE-with-logits, AdamW
lr 2e-5, batch 16, 3 epochs, 10% linear warm-up, `max_length` 256, on CPU (16 E-cores).

- **Fine-tuning on the real labels alone does not help** (0.864 vs 0.874 zero-shot): about 600 labelled pairs per fold
  are too little data.
- **Synthetic rubric labels do help.** With 2872 synthetic labels and no hand labels at all, NDCG@10 alone goes from
  0.874 to 0.893 and the blend reaches 0.914. Adding the training fold's real labels gives 0.894 alone and 0.917 in the
  blend. Training takes 187 s (synthetic only) to 772 s per fold. This is the cheapest accuracy lever found in this
  study; `scripts/ranking/finetune_cross_encoder.sh` packages it for KINA.
- **The synthetic requests are all passives, and that is where the gain is largest:** passives rise from 0.938
  (zero-shot) to 0.992 alone (section 4).

Notes:

- The RSS figures are Python processes and include the interpreter, torch and transformers. Inside the KINA JVM, ONNX
  Runtime with the model adds native memory outside the heap; `docs/DEVELOPMENT.md` records the footprint of the
  shipped int8 model.
- **Everything fits the 20 s budget for 40 candidates on 4 cores** by a wide margin; even `bge-reranker-base` stays

- **Int8 quantisation** halves latency (85 vs 165 ms median at 8 threads) at no measurable accuracy cost (NDCG@10 0.877

- **Deployment options for the cross-encoder:**
  1. In-process ONNX Runtime for Java (`com.microsoft.onnxruntime:onnxruntime`) plus a WordPiece tokenizer. No new
     container, no HTTP hop, part data never leaves the process.
  2. A separate Python service (sentence-transformers or onnxruntime). This keeps native code out of the JVM, but
     brings a second container of about 1 GB and an HTTP hop.

  Option 1 was chosen: the model is small and the graph is a standard BERT encoder.

## 9. Recommendation

KINA keeps the deterministic ranker as the primary signal and adds the MiniLM cross-encoder
(`ms-marco-MiniLM-L6-v2`) as a second signal, combined as `0.5 x ranknorm(det) + 0.5 x ranknorm(CE)`. On this set that
gives 0.913 against 0.892 (paired CI +0.003 to +0.041), with the largest gains on discretes, ICs and connectors and
without hurting passives; on vague requests it stays close to the deterministic ranker. The int8 model is 23 MB and
needs 85 ms median, 199 ms worst for a query on 8 threads, so it fits the 20 s budget many times over. This is what
ships (`docs/DESIGN.md` sections 3.3 and 3.5):

- `CrossEncoderPartRanker` behind the `PartRanker` interface, run in the KINA JVM with ONNX Runtime; the pair text is
  `candidate_text` of `scripts/research/common.py`; `RankingService` rank-normalises both signals before blending.
- The deterministic order is the fallback when the model is not ready or exceeds its time budget.
- The zero-shot int8 model is downloaded by default; `scripts/ranking/finetune_cross_encoder.sh` builds the fine-tuned
  model (0.914 to 0.917 blended in this study) in the same layout, and `CrossEncoderEvaluationTest` re-scores a model
  directory against `ranking-eval.jsonl` before it ships.

Follow-ups, each re-checked with `evaluate.py`:

1. Ship the fine-tuned cross-encoder once the labelled set grows (the 2-fold estimate for `synth_real` is 0.917).
2. Improve the deterministic ranker where the cross-encoder showed it is blind: interface type words (RS-485 vs CAN vs
   LIN), connector pin and row count (`1x4P`), TVS polarity, Zener nominal voltage when the description also lists a
   range, and vague requests ("decoupling" means 100 nF MLCC; "pull-up" means 2.2k to 10k), possibly through a small
   synonym table in `QueryParser`.
3. Keep `ranking-eval.jsonl` as a regression set: blended NDCG@10 must not drop below the deterministic ranker's.

## 10. What could not be evaluated, and caveats

- **Sample size.** 32 queries: effects below about 0.02 NDCG@10 are not resolvable (see the CIs). LCSC makes up 80 % of
  candidates because of the Mouser call cap.
- **Dataset growth.** The 9 USB connector queries added after the study are not in the committed score files; a rerun
  of the scorers on the full file covers all 41 queries and gives different averages (`scripts/research/README.md`
  shows how to score the 32-query subset).
- **Mouser calls.** 14 distinct queries were sent to the stack, and the decoupling query triggered KINA's phrase
  fallback, so 15 Mouser API calls in total. Two further stack queries were already cached and free.
- **GPU latency** was not measured (no GPU on this host); the CPU numbers are already far inside the budget.
- **Fine-tuned cross-encoder for production.** The 2-fold numbers are what to expect on new queries; a model trained on
  all 32 queries was not evaluated, because no held-out data would remain.
- **Software versions.** The committed neural scores were produced with torch 2.14 and transformers 5.18 (recorded in
  each score file's `meta`), the ONNX files with an optimum export. The research image now used by `rc.sh` is the
  fine-tuning image of `scripts/ranking/docker` (transformers 4.x, torch.onnx export); a rerun can differ in the last
  digits.
- **Label bias.** The labelling rubric is parametric by nature, which favours rule-based rankers. The cross-encoder's
  wins on non-parametric queries happened despite this.
